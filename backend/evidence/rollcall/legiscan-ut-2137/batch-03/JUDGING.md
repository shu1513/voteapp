# Utah 2025 batch-03 — how each measure was judged

Sources, in order: the Office of Legislative Research and General Counsel's
neutral summary at the top of each bill on le.utah.gov, then the enrolled text
as ground truth. Sponsor floor remarks are advocacy and were not used.

Every enrolled act was read through `ut_text.py`, which renders Utah's enrolled
XML with its change markup intact: text the act adds, text it deletes, and text
that is existing law being reprinted are three different things, and a flat text
dump loses that difference.

## HB 119 Solar Panel Restrictions in Homeowners Associations Amendments

One code section, 57-8a-701. The summary is two lines that appear to
contradict each other: the act "stops a homeowners association from prohibiting
solar panel installation" and "authorizes a homeowners association to restrict
solar panel installation". The enrolled text settles it.

Before the act, subsection (3) let a recorded declaration ban solar panels
outright on a lot with a detached house. The act deletes that subsection. What
remains is a 67 percent owner vote to add a new ban, and the act lowers the vote
needed to change a ban already in place from more than 67 percent to at least 51
percent. Restrictions short of a ban survive, but only within the existing 5
percent limits on lost output and added cost, which the act moves into the list
of things a declaration or association rule may require.

So the net is pro-solar, and the second summary line describes the limits that
were kept, not a new power. `environment_and_public_health` / for.

## HB 360 Housing Attainability Amendments

Fourteen code sections. The housing strand dominates: it opens the Utah Homes
Investment Program to cities of the first and second class, which are Utah's
largest, so they may buy and rehabilitate single-family houses and sell them
under a five-year owner-occupancy deed restriction; it moves the program's
repeal date from 2027 to 2028 and its deposit deadline from the end of 2025 to
the end of 2027; it deletes the July 1, 2026 repeal date for the Utah Housing
Corporation; it lets school districts sell, use or lease surplus land for
moderate income housing; and it lets the Department of Transportation sell
surplus land to a housing agency at pre-entitlement value with payment deferred.

Two provisions cut the other way and are named in the record for that reason.
The act widens the closed-meeting rule at 52-4-205 so that a city or county, and
not only the state, may close a meeting to discuss a proposed development on
land it owns when an open discussion would disclose the property's value or cost
it the best terms. And it removes the requirement that no more than three of the
Utah Housing Corporation's six public trustees belong to the same political
party, replacing it with a county-spread requirement.

The closed-meeting change is narrow and conditional, and the act's purpose and
weight are housing, so the record carries one label and the description states
the closed-meeting change plainly rather than burying it.
`housing_affordability` / for.

## SB 165 Municipal Broadband Service Amendments

Eighteen code sections, effectively a rewrite of Title 10, Chapter 18 to cover
broadband. Before the act that chapter reached municipal cable television and
public telecommunications service; the act adds broadband service and brings it
under the same conditions.

The conditions are real. A city must hold a preliminary public hearing, hire an
outside feasibility consultant whose study now projects ten years rather than
five, clear a revenue test, hold at least two more public hearings at least
three weeks apart rather than seven days, and adopt the study by resolution.
New subsection (4) of 10-18-302 requires voter approval at an election before a
city may issue a revenue bond for broadband, which the chapter did not require
for a cable or telecommunications revenue bond. Operating limits follow: no
cross-subsidy from other utilities, separate enterprise funds, a wall between
the staff who run the network and the staff who regulate private providers, and
public disclosure of bond defaults and rating changes.

A city whose legislative body authorized broadband before March 1, 2025 is
exempt from the pre-approval conditions in Part 2 but remains subject to the
operating limits in Part 3.

The direction is contested in good faith: supporters call it taxpayer
protection and competitive fairness. But the measurable effect is that a city
that wants to build a public network must clear steps it did not have to clear
before. `public_infrastructure` / against.

## SB 262 Housing Affordability Modifications

Ten code sections. The centerpiece is a new subordinate shared appreciation loan
program at 35A-8-504.6, run by the Department of Workforce Services through
nonprofit lenders: up to $150,000, no interest, no monthly payment, subordinate
to the first mortgage, combined loan-to-value no more than 105 percent, repaid
as principal plus a share of the home's appreciation that may not exceed the
share of the home's value the loan represented at origination. Program money
must be matched at least three to one by private funds. The department could
accept applications from nonprofit lenders only until September 1, 2025, which
is stated in the record because it bounds what the act actually does.

Secondary strands: home ownership promotion zone money may now cover water
exaction, street lighting and environmental remediation costs; a city or county
legislative body may settle a land use lawsuit by consent agreement approved in
an open meeting; and a county must apply the land use chapter to pending as well
as new applications. `housing_affordability` / for.

## SB 297 Congregate Care Amendments

Twelve code sections in Title 26B, mostly new. This is oversight of the
residential programs that house children away from home, an industry Utah hosts
in unusual concentration.

What the act requires: each program files proposed admissions criteria with each
license application and renewal, the state approves or denies them, the program
must decline a child who does not meet the approved criteria, and the state
verifies compliance at least quarterly. Each child gets a disruption plan and a
suicide prevention plan, and a list of authorized contacts. Critical incidents
must be documented and reported to the state and to the child's parent or
guardian within one business day; the statutory list of critical incidents runs
to thirteen items and includes self-harm, restraint or seclusion, a denied or
unreasonably delayed request for medical care, an unauthorized departure, use of
force in transport, and any incident or allegation of abuse or harm. A child in
crisis triggers notice to a parent within five hours. The act creates a
congregate care ombudsman who takes and investigates reports, backed by
whistleblower protection; each program must post the ombudsman's details in
one-inch type and provide a phone from which a child may call the ombudsman at
any time, without interference and in private. Background-check rules tighten
for congregate care staff.

The act also settles who pays a hospital when a child is transported there, and
creates a civil money penalty fund.

`social_programs_and_welfare` / for. The honest objection is cost and regulatory
burden on providers, which is a different axis, so the no side gets no tag.

## The one place the direction was argued both ways

SB 165 is the only measure here where a reasonable person could file the yes
vote as a win for a different area — competition policy, or protection of
municipal finances. The record does not claim otherwise; the description states
the steps the act adds and lets the reader see them.
