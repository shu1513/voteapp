## HB 4018 scores `against`, and the nay gets no stance

HB 4018 is the session's hardest direction call, and it is worth setting out.

The Act **delays** the campaign money disclosure rules and the public finance
dashboard passed in 2024, from January 1, 2028 to January 1, 2031, applying
them only to elections after January 2032. The complaint and civil penalty
changes slip from 2027 to 2031. It also specifies what the system will
eventually require, and tightens contribution limits by treating membership
organizations run by the same people as one.

`anti_corruption` is defined as preventing abuse of public office through
transparency, ethics rules and enforcement. A three-year delay to transparency
and penalty provisions cuts against that, so the **yea scores `against`**.

**The nay takes no stance, and the roll call is the reason.** The nays came
from both directions at once: reform-minded Democrats objecting to the delay
(Chaichi, Chotzen, Gamba, Gomberg, Campos, Gelser Blouin, Golden, Pham) and
others objecting to the disclosure regime itself (Cate, Diehl, Hayden). A
single nay stance would misdescribe roughly half of them, so every nay voter
gets a record of the vote and no tag.

## SB 1507 carries two labels pulling opposite ways

This is the clearest multi-strand measure in the session, and the two strands
genuinely point in different directions.

- It adds back to Oregon taxable income two federal breaks — car loan interest
  and gain on qualified small business stock — and refuses federal bonus
  depreciation. It also raises Oregon's earned income tax credit and adds a
  jobs credit. Taxing investment breaks while raising a credit for low-paid
  workers is `reduce_wealth_gap`, **for**.
- The same additions raise what Oregonians owe in state income tax. Under
  `personal_income_tax_reduction`, which is simply "lower personal income
  tax", the yea is **against** and the nay is genuinely **for**.

One label per strand, and each strand keeps its own direction.

## HB 4179 follows the 2025 SB 1173 precedent

HB 4179 treats doctors at OHSU-created nonprofits as state employees on duty,
which puts a claim against them under the Oregon Tort Claims Act and its cap on
what an injured patient may recover. Narrowing what a patient can recover cuts
against consumer protection, so the yea scores `corporate_accountability`
**against** — the same reading given to Oregon's SB 1173 in 2025. The nay takes
no stance, because opposition here is as likely to be about OHSU's structure as
about patient recovery.

## Version check, per roll

HB 4159 and HB 4179 are both recorded from the House concurrence. Each had an
earlier lopsided House vote (49-2 and 50-1) on its own text; the Senate then
amended, and the divided vote is the one on the enacted text. Both are
House-only because the Senate passed the enacted text 28-0 and 27-1.
SB 1512 is House-only (Senate 28-2).
