# Nevada batch-08 — work and criminal justice

**8 measures / 13 roll calls / 255 records / 41 candidates.** Same non-enacted scope as
batch-04, whose `PLAN.md` carries the scope argument.

Six measures passed both chambers and were vetoed. Two did not become law for other
reasons, and both descriptions say which: SB 172 passed the Senate and the Assembly amended
it but never took a passage vote, and AB 91 passed the Assembly and the Senate never voted
on it.

| measure | area | rolls | what it would have done |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AB 155 | public_education_quality / for | house 26-16, senate 16-4 | class sizes become something districts must negotiate with unions |
| AB 434 | civil_rights / for | house 27-15, senate 13-8 | employers may not punish a worker who skips a meeting pushing the employer's religion or politics |

| AB 209 | public_safety_and_crime_control / for | house 27-15, senate 14-7 | immunity from a prostitution charge for someone whose own call for help produced the evidence |
| AB 488 | civil_rights / for | house 27-15, senate 14-7 | widens a trafficking victim's right to have a conviction erased and sealed |
| AB 91 | civil_rights / for | house 27-15 | a parole second look for long sentences, and youth parole up to age 25 |

Every label carries `"nay": null`.

Three measures appear with one chamber only because the other chamber's vote was not
divided under the campaign's gate: SB 410's Assembly vote was 35-7, and SB 172 and AB 91
never got a vote in the second chamber at all.

## Two measures where the chambers voted different bills

**AB 434.** The Assembly voted a text banning any meeting held "where the purpose ... is to
communicate the opinion of the employer" on religion or politics. The Senate voted a text
that narrows this to the "**primary** purpose" and adds a list of five things the ban does
not reach, the largest being "A requirement that is limited to the managerial or supervisory
employees of an employer" — so an employer could still compel managers to attend. The
Assembly's version contains none of that list.

**AB 209.** One word, repeated four times. The Assembly voted immunity for someone who
"seeks assistance from a 911 emergency service or medical provider". The Senate voted
"seeks **medical** assistance". Under the Assembly's text a call reporting a violent assault
could trigger immunity; under the Senate's, the call must be for medical help.

Each chamber's record describes the version that chamber voted.

## Dropped after reading the text

**SB 198 (final pay after quitting or being fired) — dropped under filter 5.** It reads two
ways, and the second way is easy to miss.

It raises the penalty for late final wages, from a day's pay per day to twelve hours' pay
per day, and adds a second penalty layer. But it also invents a new category called
"compensation" — bonuses, profit sharing, fringe benefits and employer retirement
contributions — moves that category off the immediate deadline onto the ordinary payday,
and strikes it from the penalty trigger. Section 3 now reads "If an employer fails to pay
[:] wages that are earned and unpaid: (a) Within 3 days after the wages [or compensation] of
a discharged employee [becomes] become due". The bracketed "or compensation" is being
deleted. Under current law a late bonus triggers the penalty; under this bill it does not.
Higher penalties on wages set against removing a whole category from protection is the same
shape as AB 388, SB 142 and SB 160.

**AB 88 (juvenile informal supervision) — dropped under filter 5, and the reason is the
version split itself.** The Assembly voted a text that simply strikes the cross-reference to
NRS 62C.200, removing both existing conditions: the child's admission and the district
attorney's written approval. The Senate voted a text that puts the admission requirement
back, adds a new requirement that all parties agree or the court hold a hearing and make a
finding, and adds a flat bar: the court "may not refer a child to a probation officer for
informal supervision ... if the petition ... would have constituted: (a) A category A
felony; or (b) Any other felony involving the use or threatened use of force or violence."
One chamber voted to loosen the rule; the other voted a text that loosens it in one place
and tightens it in two others. There is no single direction that honestly describes both
votes.

**SB 350 (the time allowed to carry out a death sentence) — dropped under filter 3.** The
bill lengthens the window between a death warrant and the execution week. Both chambers
voted to lengthen it, and only the numbers differ: the Assembly voted 120 to 180 days, the
Senate 180 to 270, against existing law's 60 to 90. But the vote is about the length of a
scheduling window, not about whether Nevada has a death penalty, and no research area
carries a defensible direction for that. Recording it as `general` with no stance was
considered and rejected: unlike the redistricting and legislative-pay measures elsewhere in
this campaign, a scheduling interval is not something a voter would judge a legislator on.
