# How batch 04 was judged

All nine acts, the six imported and the three dropped, were read in full from the enrolled
text with the strikethrough and underline marks visible. Nothing was judged from a title or
from LegiScan's description.

**SB 942, discrimination in schools and colleges.** The act does two things that have to be
described together. It bans discrimination against students and workers in public schools and
state colleges on seven listed grounds, and it makes those bodies write the International
Holocaust Remembrance Alliance definition of anti-Semitism into their codes of conduct and
treat anti-Semitic harassment as they treat harassment based on race. It also builds the
machinery: a coordinator at the state school department and another at the college regents,
complaints, a thirty-day window to put things right, and a referral to the federal government
where a school does not. `civil_rights`, yes = for. The objection that a set definition
narrows what may be said about Israel is a free speech argument, which runs on a different
axis, so the no side is null.

**SB 991, the definition on its own.** Two sentences of substance. The state adopts the same
2016 definition, and says plainly that it is not binding law and may be used only for
training, education, spotting hate crimes and bias, and tracking incidents. A savings clause
protects First Amendment rights. `civil_rights`, yes = for, on the same reasoning as SB 942.

**SB 250, tips and the minimum wage.** The change is one clause long and easy to misread. Old
law let an employer count tips, meals or lodging toward the minimum wage but capped the credit
at half the wage, which guaranteed a tipped worker half the minimum wage in cash. The act
removes the cap and puts the federal cash wage in its place, which is $2.13 an hour. The
guaranteed cash floor therefore falls from about $3.63, half of the $7.25 minimum wage, to
$2.13. Scored `reduce_wealth_gap`, yes = **against**, following the campaign's line on minimum
wage measures. The no side is null:
the employer-side argument is about restaurant costs, not about the gap.

**SB 2084, settlements at state colleges.** The act caps at two years of pay what a
college in the state system may pay a faculty member to resolve a wrongful firing claim
brought under state law, whether by agreement, arbitration or court judgment. Federal
claims sit outside the cap, and the records say so. The statute measures the cap as base
salary, compensation or contractual benefit; the records say "pay" rather than "base pay"
so they do not understate it. Scored `government_spending_reduction`,
yes = for, because the plain effect is a ceiling on public money paid out. The competing
reading, that the cap cuts what a wrongly fired professor can recover, is real, but it belongs
to a workers' rights axis the research areas do not carry, and `legal_competence` is barred
from roll-call records. Recording it under spending keeps the direction honest.

**SB 998, electric plant costs.** The act moves cost and risk from the electric company to
its customers in three ways: a shorter decision window for gas plants, permission to bill
customers for a gas plant before it makes any power, and a new deferral that lets a company
set aside ninety percent of the yearly cost of new equipment and collect it in rates over
twenty years. The prudence review survives, and an abandoned project must be refunded with
interest, and the description says both. `corporate_accountability`, yes = **against**. The
reliability argument for the act is real but runs on a different axis, so the no side is null.

**SB 658, foster and adoptive parents.** The act bars the Department of Human Services from
requiring a foster or adoptive parent to affirm a government policy on sexual orientation or
gender identity that conflicts with their sincerely held religious or moral beliefs, from
refusing approval on those grounds, and from ruling such a parent out of a particular
placement. Scored `civil_rights`, yes = **against**, following the campaign's existing line on
religious belief protections in foster care and adoption, which are recorded that way in other
states. The no side is null, because the religious liberty argument is a civil rights claim
running the other way on the same axis and no honest single direction covers both.

## The three that were read and dropped

- **SB 680** taxes heated tobacco as a cigarette and then forgives half the tax. Taxing a
  product that escaped the tax and writing a discount for it are both fair readings of the
  same section, so no direction is defensible.
- **SB 641** caps body shop administrative and storage charges and makes insurers reimburse
  the capped rates. Whichever area it is filed under, one clause protects the payer and the
  next protects the shop.
- **SB 1365** exempts Tourism Department gift shop stock from central purchasing up to
  $75,000. Too narrow a subject, and the oversight argument cuts both ways.

## Things worth recording

- **Two acts in one session adopt the same definition of anti-Semitism.** SB 991 adopts it as
  a general tool; SB 942 puts it into school and college conduct codes with an enforcement
  path. Different acts, different days, different tallies, so both are recorded. The campaign's
  duplicate test is the same tally, not the same subject.
- **The emergency clause explains a version diff.** SB 942 and SB 998 lost their emergency
  section and their July 1 effective date between engrossment and enrollment, because the
  House emergency vote failed in each case. A reader who did not check the emergency roll
  might take that for a substantive change.
- Four of six imported measures were signed; two became law without the Governor's signature.
  Across four Oklahoma batches, checking how a measure actually became law has mattered every
  time.
