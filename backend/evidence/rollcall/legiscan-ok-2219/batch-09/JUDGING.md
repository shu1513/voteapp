# How batch 09 was judged

This batch comes from a review of the Oklahoma calls most open to question. Each act was read
again from the enrolled text with the strikethrough and underline marks visible, and each call
was checked against how other states' versions of the same kind of law are recorded.

## Changed

**SB 1027, citizen petitions. Brought back from the drop list.** The act makes the summary on
each signature page plain, neutral and honest about cost, and lets the Secretary of State
reject one that is not. People gathering signatures must be registered Oklahoma voters, must
say whether they are paid and by whom, and may not be paid by the signature. Only people and
groups who live or do business in Oklahoma may pay for signature gathering, and spenders report
every week. Signers may ask to be taken off. Signatures from any one county count only up to
11.5 percent of that county's votes for Governor for a new law, or 20.8 percent for a change to
the constitution.

It was first dropped because the honesty rules and the limits on who may take part seemed to
pull opposite ways inside `election_integrity`. On review, every strand already has a
precedent under that area, yes = for. Colorado HB 1320 required plain ballot titles. Florida
HB 1205 required petition gatherers to register and live in the state. Missouri SB 152 and
Montana HB 818 barred foreign money from ballot measure campaigns. The objection that the act
makes petitions harder to qualify is real, but no research area covers access to direct
democracy. It runs on a different axis, as it did for Florida, so the no side is null. The
description names the county limits plainly so a voter can weigh them.

**SB 2084, settlements at state colleges.** Now `labor_rights`, yes = against. The act caps what
a wrongly fired professor can recover at two years of pay. That is a limit on a worker's remedy,
the core of the Labor Rights area. The earlier spending label was chosen only because that area
did not yet exist, and the cap touches no budget.

**HB 3127, medical marijuana at work.** Adds `labor_rights`, yes = against, beside
`civil_rights`, yes = against. The act lets any employer with a written testing policy act on a
positive test, where before only safety-sensitive jobs were exempt. That narrows a job
protection. Iowa HF 767, which widened employer drug testing, carries `labor_rights`, against.
Maryland SB 439, which protected medical cannabis users at work, carries `civil_rights`. Both
labels point the same way, so keeping both loses nothing.

## Reviewed and kept

**SB 743, disturbing worship.** Kept as `public_safety_and_crime_control`, yes = for. The act
raises penalties and makes a repeat offense a felony. Its eight-foot rule within 100 feet of
an entrance limits speech on a public sidewalk, which is a civil rights question, but that
question pulls both ways: the rule protects people going to worship and restricts people
speaking outside. So it takes no civil rights label, and every record describes the rule.

**SB 658, foster parents' beliefs.** Kept as `civil_rights`, yes = against. The same kind of law
is recorded that way in three states: Iowa SF 473, Montana HB 655 and Kansas HB 2311.

**SB 20, commercial drivers.** Kept as `immigration`, yes = against. Laws that tie a license or a
job to immigration status are recorded that way: Idaho H 667, Wyoming HB 116 and Tennessee
SB 1587. The Labor Rights area leaves out work-status checks such as E-Verify on purpose.

**SB 1443, anesthesia payment.** Kept as `corporate_accountability`, yes = for. The act tells
health plans how to pay for anesthesia for very sick patients. As healthcare affordability it
runs both ways, because higher provider pay means higher premiums, and it would have been
dropped.
