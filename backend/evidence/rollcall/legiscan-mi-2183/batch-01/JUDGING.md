# Michigan batch-01 — judging

Every description was written from the version of the measure that became law.
The sources were the Michigan Public Act text and the nonpartisan analysis
published by the House Fiscal Agency or the Senate Fiscal Agency for the
enrolled version. No description was written from a bill title, a sponsor
statement, or a LegiScan summary.

## The version check, run for every roll

Michigan substitutes are lettered: H-1, S-3, and so on. For each of the eight
rolls the question was resolved from the bill's own history, and the document
that chamber voted was named before a word was written.

Two of the eight are concurrence votes, where the chamber accepted the other
chamber's substitute. Michigan gives those votes no question at all in the vote
description — the text is only a roll-call number — so the question comes from
the history line immediately before the roll-call line.

- **HB 4350**, House 2026-07-03: the history line before the roll reads
  `Senate Substitute (s-1) Concurred In`. The S-1 is the enacted text, and it
  is the one described. The House's own earlier version passed 82-24 on
  2025-05-14 and is not in this batch, because it is not the text that became
  law.
- **SB 81**, Senate 2026-07-03: `House Substitute (h-1) Concurred In`. The
  Senate's earlier 25-12 vote of 2025-03-18 is likewise not used.

The other six are passage votes on text no later version replaced.

## The measures

### HB 4064 — anti_corruption, a yes vote is for
Public Act text confirms the act bars a sitting legislator from taking
compensation to lobby, and that the ban is not limited to Michigan. The House
Fiscal Agency analysis places it in a package of three bills: HB 4062 and
HB 4063 extend a two-year ban to former legislators and former executive
officers, and HB 4064 is the one reaching sitting members. Only HB 4064 has a
closely divided final vote, so only it is described.

**Watch this if the batch is revisited.** Three hand-written records describe a
House vote on HB 4064 taken on 2025-02-20, 103-6. That is a different roll from
the Senate vote imported here, so those records are not duplicates.

### HB 4350 — environment_and_public_health, a yes vote is against
The act lets a person feed wild birds and wildlife within 300 feet of a home,
with no more than two gallons in total counting feed already present,
notwithstanding the Natural Resources Commission's order stopping deer and elk
feeding.

**The threshold comes from the enacted text, not the analysis.** The House
Fiscal Agency summary says "less than two gallons". Enacted section
40111a(1)(b) says "not more than 2 gallons", and adds the feed "previously
placed ... and still present" to the count. Exactly two gallons is allowed.
The description follows the statute. Lesson for later batches: the Fiscal
Agency summary is the guide to what changed, but every number and threshold
must be checked against the enacted section.

The direction rests on what the Commission's order is for. The House Fiscal
Agency analysis quotes the Governor's 2022 veto of the nearly identical
HB 4088: the feeding ban "is based on strong scientific evidence, which
demonstrates that feeding disrupts normal wildlife movement patterns, causing
animals to congregate and thereby accelerating the spread of deadly diseases
like bovine tuberculosis." Loosening that ban points against public health, so
a yes vote is against.

The description says only what the act does and that any feeding order must
follow federal disease-control standards. It does not describe the science,
because that is the Commission's judgment and not the act's text. Subsection (2)
of the enacted section was read directly to confirm that the federal-standards
requirement binds the Commission's **order**, not the person feeding.

### HB 4746 — social_programs_and_welfare, a yes vote is for
The Senate Fiscal Agency analysis and the enacted section 400.14n agree: the
department must issue a chip-enabled Bridge Card to every food or cash
assistance recipient within one year, meet published industry standards, and
keep adding later security features.

The description does not say why chip cards matter, because the analysis does
not say so and a reason not in the source is not evidence.

### HB 4951 — public_infrastructure, a yes vote is for
The act creates the Comprehensive Road Funding Tax Act, a 24 percent excise tax
on wholesale marijuana sales and transfers, with nearly all revenue going to a
new Neighborhood Roads Fund. The House Fiscal Agency's package analysis puts the
yield at about $420 million a year.

The description renders 24 percent as "24 cents on the dollar", which is the
same figure in plainer words.

This bill is tie-barred to HB 4183, HB 4961 and HB 4968, and it is part of the
2025 transportation funding package. Only HB 4951 is described, and only for its
own contents.

**The topic reads two ways and the area choice is deliberate.** A new tax could
be filed under a tax-reduction area, where a yes vote would look like a vote
against taxpayers. It is filed under `public_infrastructure` instead, because on
that topic the direction is clean: the act dedicates new money to road work.
This follows the practice of trying another research area before dropping a
measure whose first area reads both ways.

### SB 81 — civil_rights, a yes vote is for
The act extends the Michigan Indigent Defense Commission to young people in the
juvenile justice system who cannot afford a lawyer, requires minimum standards
for how local governments deliver that defense, sets out how standards may be
changed, and revises the Commission's membership.

Effective assistance of counsel is a right, so widening who receives it is
`civil_rights` with a yes vote for.

### SB 293 and SB 294 — public_safety_and_crime_control, a yes vote is for
The two acts make the same change to two sections of the Michigan Penal Code:
section 50, animal neglect or cruelty, and section 50b, torturing or killing an
animal. Under the old law a seized animal could be forfeited before trial only
if the prosecutor won a separate civil action. The acts add the notice-and-bond
process already used for animal fighting (section 49): the animal control
agency serves notice, and the owner has 14 days to post a security deposit
covering the animal's care or request a hearing; missing that deadline, or
failing to post after a hearing, forfeits the animal without any conviction.
Acquittal returns the animal and any unused deposit. The separate civil action
is kept as an alternative route when the notice process is not used. A person
convicted can be ordered to pay restitution covering the investigation and
prosecution and the animal's seizure, care, housing, veterinary treatment and
disposition.

An earlier draft of the descriptions said the forfeiture question is "now
settled in the criminal case". That was wrong: the main new path is forfeiture
by deadline before the criminal case ends. The descriptions now say so.

Each act is described in its own terms, naming its own offense, so a reader is
not told the two records are about the same thing.

**The countervailing reading was considered.** The bond-or-forfeit process
lets an owner lose an animal on a missed deadline, before any finding of guilt.
That is a real cost to owners. It does not flip the direction, because the
process is the one the Penal Code already applies to animal fighting, the owner
keeps the right to a hearing before a judge, and an acquitted owner gets the
animal back. The direction stays for, and the descriptions state the
before-conviction forfeiture plainly so a reader can weigh it.
