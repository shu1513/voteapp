# North Carolina batch-01: the bills enacted over the governor's veto

## Why this batch

North Carolina has a Republican legislature and a Democratic governor. That
split produces a pool of votes no single-party state has: the veto override.
An override needs three fifths of the members present, so every override is
divided by definition, and an override that carries makes the bill law.

The 2025-2026 session has 26 override rolls on 14 bills. Twelve of those bills
were overridden in both chambers and became session laws. Two were overridden in
one chamber only and stayed vetoed, so they are outside the standard gate.

Override rolls are also the cleanest rolls to judge. Both chambers vote the same
ratified bill, so the version check that costs the most work in other states -
which text did this chamber actually vote on - is answered by the procedure
itself.

## How the batch was chosen

The campaign's five filters, in order:

1. Divided: the smaller side is at least a quarter of the larger.
2. Consequential: the bill became law.
3. A nameable subject that maps to one of the research areas.
4. One roll per measure per chamber. For an overridden bill the override roll is
   the chamber's final action, so it is always the roll to take.
5. A defensible direction. A measure whose text runs both ways is dropped rather
   than recorded with a stance it cannot support.

Seven of the twelve measures passed all five filters.

Six of the seven score against their area. That is what the pool is: the
override set is the legislative majority's agenda passed over the governor's
objection. It is the mirror image of the single-party states in this campaign,
where every divided and enacted measure scored for its area.

## What was dropped, and why

- House Bill 87, Educational Choice for Children Act. The state opts into a
  federal tax credit for donations to scholarship granting organizations. This
  is school-choice financing, which no area carries an honest direction on
  (Texas Senate Bill 2 and Georgia Senate Bill 82 precedent). Expect to be asked
  about this one.
- Senate Bill 254, Charter School Changes. Moves charter school rulemaking from
  the State Board of Education to the Charter School Review Board and adds
  improvement plans for low-performing charters. Reduces one kind of oversight
  and adds another.
- House Bill 402, Limit Rules With Substantial Financial Costs. Requires a law
  to approve any agency rule costing $20 million over five years and a
  supermajority of a board to adopt cheaper ones. This is not government
  efficiency, which means better service delivery: it blocks rules, and the
  legislature's own analysis flags separation-of-powers questions.
- House Bill 549, Clarify Powers of State Auditor. Expands some of the auditor's
  powers while deleting audits the office was required to perform, and exempts
  the office from statutes that apply to other agencies.
- Senate Bill 416, Personal Privacy Protection Act. Bars public agencies from
  releasing the names of nonprofit donors. Reads as data privacy in one
  direction and as hiding political money in the other.

## What was held out

Three House override rolls of 2026-06-24, on Senate Bills 153, 227 and 558, are
held at pending. LegiScan's member list for those rolls omits the chamber's
unaffiliated members, so the feed shows 71-46 where the official transcript
records 71-47. See `../CODE-FINDINGS.md`. Their Senate rolls are in the batch,
so those three measures are covered on the Senate side only.
